Why listen to me

Why this is called surviving — not thriving.

My last two years at work have swung between nearly being performance-managed out and landing a top-performer bonus — twice each. The current half is still unwritten.

So these lessons aren't from someone who always wins and never had to learn why. They're from someone who fails, recovers, fails again, and recovers again.
The premise

The rules changed.
Did you?

AI agents can now write code, run pipelines, analyze data, and ship features. The question is no longer can you do the work — it's why should it be you?

Surviving as an Engineer

Identity

Your title is not your identity. Your problem is.

Stop thinking of yourself as a "Data Engineer" or "Backend Engineer." Start thinking: "I am a problem solver for Stakeholder X."

  • Your stack will change. Your stakeholders' problems won't.
  • No one retains an engineer for their platform preference.
  • They retain you for what you made easier, faster, or possible.
"Your scope of work is not X Engineer. It's Problem Solver for X."
The baseline

Technical skill is the floor, not the ceiling.

You cannot fail on technical. But you cannot survive on technical alone.

Bare minimum · you can't skip thisSurvival skill · what sets you apart
Strong fundamentalsStakeholder empathy
Clean, working codeClear communication
Knowing your stackUnderstanding business context
Problem debuggingAnticipating needs before they're asked
Guardrails

You're no longer building just for humans. Build accordingly.

Old worldAgentic era
User clicks through a UI flowAgent calls your API directly
Frontend validates the inputNo frontend. No guarantee.
Contract enforced by UXOnly as good as your guardrail
Errors are human-readableErrors may never be seen

Even a service caller can't be trusted: a contract promises "no duplicates," a vibecoded feature ships — and now you're getting them. No one is watching.

  • Validate at the boundary — every input, every time.
  • Deduplicate defensively, even if the contract says not to.
  • Fail loudly — surfaced errors beat silent corruption.
  • Design for the worst caller, not the best one.
Don't trust the contract. Trust your guardrail.
Your moat

Guardrails aren't overhead. They're your moat.

The platforms that survive agentic adoption are the ones that didn't break when agents arrived. Guardrails used to be best practice — now they're table stakes.

Input guardrails

Validate schema, types, ranges, and business rules at every API boundary.

Don't assume the caller is a well-behaved human.

Idempotency & dedup

Handle repeated or duplicate calls gracefully.

Vibecoded agents will retry — and send the same payload twice.

Observability

If an agent breaks your service silently, you need to know.

Log, alert, trace every integration point.

Strong fundamentals + guardrails = a platform agents can use without destroying.
Reality check

If it's purely technical, AI can do it — and it's getting better.

This isn't a threat. It's a redirect.

→ Pure technical execution  =  commoditized
→ Technical + context + relationship  =  valuable

The engineers who thrive don't out-code the AI. They know which problem to solve and why it matters.

Retention

Your customer's satisfaction is your retention letter.

When your stakeholder is happy, you are kept. When they're confused or blocked — no matter how elegant your code — you are a liability.

  1. 1Find out what your stakeholders care about most.
  2. 2Document what you built and how it helped them.
  3. 3Get their testimony, not just their sign-off.
"Anda puas, saya dipertahankan."Your satisfaction = my job security.

Surviving as an Employee

Agency

Everyone is replaceable. So act like it — in a good way.

Not irreplaceable through gatekeeping. High-agency: the person who moves things forward without being told.

Low agency

Waits for direction.

Protects their lane.

Defined by the job description.

Medium agency

Follows and improves.

Works within their lane.

Does the job description.

High agency

Creates direction.

Extends their lane when needed.

Exceeds it with context.

Durability

You are not your job title. You are your ability to learn.

"I am an accountant" is a fragile identity when the role changes. "I learn fast and adapt" is durable.

  • An HR professional who learns to use AI for screening  →  retained
  • An accountant who automates their own reporting  →  valued
  • A data analyst who coaches their team on AI tools  →  promoted
Attach your identity to how you grow — not what you do.
Environment

Your manager is your environment. Choose wisely.

A good manager amplifies your impact. A bad one absorbs it.

  1. 1Try to help them improve — without lecturing.
  2. 2If they can't be influenced, build impact wider than their scope.
  3. 3Let your work speak to people above, beside, and beyond them.
Build your impact wider than your manager's shadow.
Ecosystem

Stakeholders aren't your audience. They're your ecosystem.

A stakeholder is anyone whose work touches your scope — or whose scope your work touches.

  • Don't be a blocker. Be a connector.
  • Get reviews. Document outcomes. Collect testimony.
  • Be the person they think of when things need to get done.
Stakeholder trust is the currency that outlasts any role, stack, or company.

Surviving as an Individual

Beginner's mind

Your past experience isn't the advantage. Your willingness to start fresh is.

Accumulated expertise gives you context — but it also gives you blind spots. The most dangerous trap: thinking you already know how things work.

  • Treat every new AI tool as a new discipline to learn.
  • Unlearn before you re-learn.
  • Bring your domain wisdom — leave your method attachment at the door.
The barrier to a new industry isn't expertise. It's willingness.
Where to build

When barriers are this low, where you build matters more than ever.

One idea underneath all of this
Let go of attachment.
Let go of your platform  ·  become a problem solver
Let go of your job title  ·  become a learner
Let go of your past expertise  ·  become a beginner again
Let go of fast, easy markets  ·  build where it's slow and deep
The cost

Attachment is the hidden reason people get left behind.

The attached engineer

"I only do Stack X. That's my expertise."

→ AI does Stack X better every quarter.
→ They wait to be replaced.

The attached employee

"My job is Role Y. That's all I'm responsible for."

→ AI takes Role Y.
→ No adjacent skills to fall back on.

The adaptive professional

"I solve problems for People Z. Let me learn whatever that takes."

→ AI becomes their tool, not their replacement.
→ They remain essential.

but this world is not made for you."
